The Through Line

I treated the assignment like a product discovery sprint.

The goal was not to prove that soccer data is interesting. The goal was to show how video analysis becomes a usable coaching tool: who it helps, what it should ignore for now, what the first prototype can prove, and what has to change before production.

Step 1

Start With The People Making Decisions

The primary users are coaches and analysts. Players matter too, but in a real club the analyst often sits between raw data and the way a coach or player receives it.

That shaped the product thesis: the problem is not a lack of numbers. It is the gap between match events, patterns across 90 minutes, and the plain-English recommendations a coach can trust.

Today Manual tagging, fragmented data, slow review
Need Actionable insight linked to exact video moments
Success Useful at halftime, pre-match prep, and team review

Step 2

Find The Opening In The Market

I compared established tools with AI-first products and kept coming back to the same product gap.

Legacy Pro Tools

Powerful and data rich, but built for clubs with analyst teams, budgets, and workflow overhead.

AI-First Tools

Accessible and useful for clips or heatmaps, but still leave the tactical conclusion to a person.

SoccerVision

Detect what happened, explain what it means, and show exactly where it happened in the footage.

Step 6

Choose A POC Architecture That Proves The Loop

The prototype keeps video processing lightweight: the browser pulls keyframes, sends them to a serverless API, Claude Vision returns structured analysis, and the dashboard renders the result.

This proves the important loop without pretending it is the final production architecture.

  1. Upload MP4, WebM, or MOV
  2. Extract keyframes with Canvas API
  3. Send JPEG frames to serverless route
  4. Analyze frames with Claude Vision
  5. Aggregate into MatchAnalysis JSON
  6. Render coaching dashboard in Next.js

Under The Hood

What The Demo Does

Five stages run every time you upload a clip. Each one does a specific job, then passes its output to the next.

  1. Keyframes captured in the browser. The full video never leaves your device. One frame is pulled every 4–6 seconds at high resolution — a deliberate choice, because the ball becomes too small to detect at half the size.
  2. Four frames analyzed at once. Each frame goes to Claude Vision with instructions to locate every player from both teams, the ball, possession, and any match events. Four run in parallel so total time is a quarter of what it would be back-to-front.
  3. Camera window calibrated. A separate AI call estimates which slice of the pitch is visible in each frame — left box, center, full-pitch wide. Without this, the same screen position means different things at different zoom levels.
  4. Key moments reviewed. The system pre-flags what is worth checking using geometry: ball near the goal line, players clustering near goal, sudden direction reversals. Claude verifies those specific windows. Scoreboards are also read in every frame and compared — a goal is confirmed the moment the number goes up, even if no visual goal cue was caught.
  5. Coaching insights generated. One call to Claude's most capable model receives the full picture: match statistics, shot positions, and up to 12 curated frames from the clip. It returns plain-English coaching insights, a win/draw/loss projection, and expected goals grounded in what it can actually see — keeper position, shot angle, defenders in the way.

Why It Takes Time

Honest About Latency

A 2-minute clip takes 3–7 minutes to process. That is not a bug — it is the cost of calling an AI API many times in sequence. Each call carries 8–15 seconds of network and model time.

Running four requests at once already cuts total time by 75% compared to doing them one after another. The remaining wait comes from the number of rounds: frame analysis, camera calibration, event review, and synthesis are each a separate trip to the API.

The Phase 2 architecture solves this by moving detection to a local GPU model. Claude is called once at the end on aggregated data, not once per frame. That is where processing time drops from minutes to seconds.

With Access To More Data

What Becomes Possible

Player ETL Named player tracking

Jersey number recognition plus a roster feed turns anonymous dots into named players. Heatmaps and sprint data per individual across a full season.

League ETL Cross-match patterns

Each analysis outputs structured data. Run it across a season and aggregate by team or competition — pressing tendency home vs away, shot zones, defensive line shifts over time.

Formation Automatic shape detection

Formation is currently fixed. A full 90-minute feed provides enough positional data to infer formation automatically and track mid-game shifts.

Live Real-time coaching alerts

The local detection worker already exists. Swap uploaded clips for a live feed and a message queue and the same stack delivers alerts in real time.

Step 7

Name The Hard Parts

Football video analysis is difficult. I would rather earn trust by showing the constraints than overpromise a magical model.

01

Homography

Camera motion and cuts make screen coordinates hard to map to real pitch coordinates.

02

Occlusion

Players overlap constantly, especially in set pieces and midfield pressure.

03

Real Time

Live analysis needs low latency, high throughput, and persistent infrastructure.

04

Accuracy vs. Speed

The right production model must be fast enough to process a match and accurate enough to trust.

Step 8

Use The Right AI At The Right Layer

Claude Vision is the right POC choice because it gets from video to structured insight quickly without training data or custom infrastructure. It is not the final answer for production tracking.
